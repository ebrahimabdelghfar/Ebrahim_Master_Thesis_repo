\begin{abstract}
On-track system identification promises tire models obtained from ordinary
racing laps instead of dedicated steady-state manoeuvres. This paper studies
the identifiability of one such pipeline---the residual-network plus
virtual-steady-state-data method of Dikici \emph{et al.}~\cite{ontrack2024}---at
full vehicle scale, and reports a corrected and extended identification
pipeline together with the simulation architecture that makes it verifiable.
The plant is a Formula Student vehicle simulated in CARLA and driven through a
purpose-built ROS~2 bridge that exposes the simulator's own per-wheel slip
angle, normal load and lateral force, so that an on-track estimator can be
scored against the forces the plant actually generated---a comparison that a
physical vehicle cannot provide without a force-measuring hub. Transplanted
unchanged, the pipeline converges on a tire whose peak grip is \SI{0.40}{\g},
with five of eight Pacejka coefficients resting on their box constraints, for
a vehicle that measures \SIrange{1.00}{1.05}{\g}. We show that this is neither
a solver, a bounds nor a tuning failure but a loss of excitation created by the
pipeline's own virtual-data step: the friction the synthetic rollout can demand
is $\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}=v^{2}\delta_{\mathrm{sweep}}/(gL)$, a property of the
rollout configuration and not of the tire, capped at $0.43$ by a rollout speed
inherited from a 1:10 platform. The paper contributes (i) this identifiability
bound and an excitation-aware rollout that removes the degeneracy; (ii) an
extrapolation bound on the synthetic steering sweep together with a
frozen-prior Tikhonov term, which jointly stop the peak factor from drifting
across co-identification iterations; (iii) three measurement-side corrections
(achieved rather than commanded road-wheel angle, measured mass and geometry,
and a peak-rather-than-median friction warm-start applied as a floor); (iv) a
ROS~2 simulation architecture that closes the identification loop end to end,
from data acquisition through fitting to ground-truth force validation; and (v)
admissibility gates on derived physical quantities, placed at the
model-to-controller boundary. After correction, three consecutive live
identifications return no coefficient on a bound, peak friction coefficients of
\numrange{1.05}{1.18}, axle cornering stiffnesses of
\SI{21}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} front and \SI{20}{\kilo\newton\per\radian}
rear against a simulator ground truth of
\SIrange{17}{19}{\kilo\newton\per\radian}, and one-step prediction $R^{2}$ of
\num{0.97} for lateral velocity and \num{0.99} for yaw rate. The corrected
identifier also makes a negative result visible: the optimised raceline used in
this study demands \SI{1.22}{\g} and is therefore infeasible for this vehicle,
and the admissibility gate rejects deployment---correctly.
\end{abstract}
