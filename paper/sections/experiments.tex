% === VI. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS =============================================
\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}

All experiments run in simulation on the architecture of
Section~\ref{sec:platform}: the Formula AI vehicle in CARLA~\cite{carla2017},
driven through on a ROS~2 Humble , with the CARLA server in
synchronous mode at a \SI{0.033}{\second} fixed physics step. Identification
data is collected over \SI{30}{\second} of pure-pursuit driving at
\SI{50}{\hertz}, matching the baseline's data budget~\cite{ontrack2024}. Ground
truth for forces, slip angles and normal loads is the simulator's own per-wheel
telemetry.

\subsubsection{Reference trajectory}
\label{subsubsec:raceline}
Every run follows the same optimised raceline (Fig.~\ref{fig:raceline}),
produced with the TUM global race trajectory optimization
~\cite{tumracetraj} under its minimum-curvature
formulation~\cite{heilmeier2020} on a recorded centreline of the CARLA
silverstone circuit: a closed \SI{5830}{\meter} lap of 2916 waypoints
at \SI{2.0}{\meter} spacing, reference speed
\SIrange{14.90}{46.02}{\meter\per\second}, minimum corner radius
\SI{18.7}{\meter} ($|\kappa|_{\max}=\SI{0.0536}{\per\meter}$).

Two of the optimiser's inputs matter for Section~\ref{subsec:negative}. It was
given a vehicle mass of \SI{240}{\kilo\gram}---the configured body mass, not
the \SI{269.8}{\kilo\gram} the wheels carry (Table~\ref{tab:vehicle})---and its
\texttt{ggv} envelope declares a \emph{constant} lateral capability of
\SI{5.0}{\meter\per\second\squared} at every speed. The resulting profile
saturates that envelope exactly---peak demand $\max(v_{x}^{2}|\kappa|)$ over
the lap is \SI{5.00}{\meter\per\second\squared}, i.e.\ \SI{0.56}{\g}---so the
trajectory is feasible \emph{by construction} for the vehicle the optimiser was
told about, and only for that vehicle.

\subsection{Peak-friction warm start}
\label{subsec:mu}

The friction warm start of Section~\ref{subsec:friction} is evaluated on a
synthetic single-track plant with brush tires rather than in CARLA first,
because only a synthetic plant supplies a $\mu$ that is known exactly and an
excitation level that can be swept independently of it. The plant is driven by
a swept-sine steering input for \SI{40}{\second} at \SI{50}{\hertz} with true
$\mu=1.05$ and $C_{\alpha}=12.1F_{z}$, and the steering amplitude sets how far
up the tire curve the manoeuvre reaches. Table~\ref{tab:mu} reports what each
estimator returns.

At \SI{45}{\percent} utilisation---a level an ordinary lap reaches---the
utilisation bound is \SI{55}{\percent} low while the brush fit is within
\SI{0.2}{\percent}. Across true $\mu\in\{0.7,1.05,1.4\}$ the fit stays within

designed: below the \SI{40}{\percent} floor it suppresses the fit and reports
the bound, so the estimator's failure is visible rather than silent.
Figures~\ref{fig:mufit}--\ref{fig:mucrosscheck} are the corresponding
diagnostics on the CARLA plant and are not yet collected.

\subsection{Identification cost}
\label{subsec:runtime}

Section~\ref{subsec:s4cost}'s claim is that the temporal residual is affordable
on the hardware a vehicle carries; Table~\ref{tab:runtime} is the measurement,
on the same synthetic \SI{30}{\second} lap and the same six co-identification
iterations throughout. Every step was verified against the implementation it
replaced before being kept: the convolutional and recurrent paths of
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipelines4}(c) agree to $<10^{-9}$ in double precision (pinned by
a regression test), the rewritten physics-informed loss is bit-identical, and
the full six-iteration loop lands on the same Pacejka coefficients to four
decimal places---the remaining difference is float32 reordering over
\num{1200} optimiser steps.

Two consequences are worth stating. First, at \SI{20.9}{\second} the
identification fits inside the \SI{30}{\second} re-identification period of
Section~\ref{subsec:deployment}, so the temporal residual is a deployable
option rather than an offline one; at \SI{181.4}{\second} it was not. Second,
the device split is not incidental: the stage-2 rollout is strictly sequential
and single-sample, so it is \num{4.4}$\times$ \emph{faster} on a CPU, while
full-batch training over $\approx\num{3000}$ windows remains
\num{2.4}$\times$ faster on the GPU. A pipeline that pins itself to one device
pays one of those two penalties.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{subsec:ablation}

Three ablation axes are exposed by the implementation and are marked as not yet
run rather than reported with placeholder numbers. The first is the residual
architecture (\texttt{baseline} MLP, \texttt{wide}, \texttt{physics\_inputs},
\texttt{ensemble}, \texttt{s4}), scored on one-step and 10-step RMSE and on the
identified coefficients (Fig.~\ref{fig:archablation}); the relevant comparison
is~\cite{visionsysid2026}, which reports a lateral-force RMSE reduction
exceeding \SI{60}{\percent} for an S4 residual, and we expect a smaller effect
here because our sequences are short and our residual is dominated by a
correctable measurement bias rather than by unmodelled temporal dynamics. The
cost objection to running this axis is now removed
(Section~\ref{subsec:runtime}): the S4D arm costs \SI{20.9}{\second} per
identification rather than \SI{181.4}{\second}. The second is the loss mode
(\texttt{mse} versus the physics-informed objective of
Section~\ref{subsec:pinnloss}) on the same dataset, comparing one-step metrics,
per-iteration coefficient trajectories and closed-loop tracking; the term of
interest is the symmetry penalty, since it is the one that supplies data the
lap does not contain. The third is the input vector: whether the slip-angle
augmentation of Section~\ref{subsec:physinputs} buys anything once the residual
is temporal, which is a question about whether $\alpha_{f},\alpha_{r}$ are
recoverable from a \SI{0.4}{\second} window (Fig.~\ref{fig:physinputs}).

\subsection{adaptation to time-varying friction}
\label{subsec:varyingmu}

The experiments reported above hold the surface fixed and ask whether the tire
model is recovered correctly. The complementary question---whether the
identifier tracks a surface that \emph{changes} while the vehicle is on
it---is evaluated with the three friction schedules introduced by
LLA-MPC~\cite{llampc2025}, reproduced here on the CARLA plant so that the
comparison is on the same axes as the adaptive-racing literature. Friction is
varied by rewriting the per-wheel \texttt{tire\_friction} of the vehicle's
physics control at the simulator's fixed \SI{0.033}{\second} step, so that the
schedule is exact and known, while the identifier sees only IMU, odometry and
steering feedback as before. In every case the plant's own per-wheel telemetry
supplies the true $\mu(t)$ against which the estimate is scored, and the same
re-identification period of Section~\ref{subsec:deployment} is used
throughout.

\subsubsection{Gradual friction decay (Experiment 1)}
The actual friction coefficient decays linearly at \SI{2}{\percent} per
second, as in~\cite{llampc2025}. This is the benign case: the surface evolves
slowly enough that a controller with a stale model degrades gradually rather
than failing outright, so it measures tracking latency rather than recovery.
The quantity of interest is the lag between $\mu(t)$ and the identified
$D(t)$, and whether the floor-only warm-start policy of
Section~\ref{subsec:frictionfloor} biases the estimate upward when grip is falling
monotonically---a case the floor was never designed for, and the one place
where the policy is expected to cost accuracy.

\subsubsection{Sudden friction drop (Experiment 2)}
A step reduction of \SI{40}{\percent} in the friction coefficient at the end
of the first lap, again following~\cite{llampc2025}. Physically this stands
for a dry-to-wet transition, an oil or debris patch, or a change of surface
material; none of these leave time for gradual adaptation. The step is placed
after one completed lap so that every method has a full lap of data before it
occurs. What is measured is the recovery time---the interval from the step to
the first identification whose $D$ is within \SI{5}{\percent} of the new
truth---and the closed-loop cost paid in the interim.

Reporting is on the three metrics used in~\cite{llampc2025}---lap time,
maximum cross-track error and a violation count---plus the friction-tracking
error $|\hat{\mu}(t)-\mu(t)|$, which is the identifier-level quantity this
work is actually about. Figures~\ref{fig:mutracking}
and~\ref{fig:mutrajectories} are the corresponding diagnostics. All three
experiments are closed-loop and therefore inherit the prerequisite of
Section~\ref{subsec:negative}: they are blocked until the raceline is
regenerated for the measured vehicle, since a trajectory already infeasible at
nominal grip cannot be driven at \SI{60}{\percent} of it.

\subsection{Negative result: the raceline, not the model}
\label{subsec:negative}

With the identifier corrected, level 3 of the validation protocol
(Section~\ref{subsec:validation}) does \emph{not} complete, and the reason is
instructive. The identified model reports a grip ceiling of
\SIrange{1.02}{1.16}{\g}; the plant sustains $\approx\SI{1.0}{\g}$ in steady
state; the raceline demands
$\max(v_{x}^{2}|\kappa|)=\SI{12.00}{\meter\per\second\squared}=\SI{1.22}{\g}$
($P_{99}=\SI{11.90}{\meter\per\second\squared}$). The admissibility gate of
Section~\ref{sec:integration} therefore rejects every identification, and does
so correctly: the trajectory is infeasible for this vehicle, and the corrected
identifier is what made that visible.

The cause is worth naming because any model-based stack can inherit it. The
trajectory optimiser was handed a \texttt{ggv} envelope asserting a constant
\SI{12.0}{\meter\per\second\squared} of lateral capability and a vehicle mass
of \SI{204}{\kilo\gram}, and it did its job---the profile saturates that
envelope to the last digit. The envelope was not this vehicle's. Identification
and trajectory generation were parameterised from two different descriptions of
the same car, and nothing in the stack compared them until the gate did.

We do not loosen the gate. The quantitative consequence for regeneration is
that the tightest corner, $|\kappa|=\SI{0.0536}{\per\meter}$, permits
$\sqrt{g\mu/|\kappa|}=\SI{13.5}{\meter\per\second}$ at the measured grip
(\SIrange{13.7}{14.6}{\meter\per\second} at the identified ceiling of
\SIrange{1.02}{1.16}{\g}); capping the speed profile at
\SI{13}{\meter\per\second} brings peak demand to
\SI{9.05}{\meter\per\second\squared} (\SI{0.92}{\g}), whereas
\SI{15}{\meter\per\second} returns it to \SI{12.00}{\meter\per\second\squared}.
Regenerating the raceline for the \emph{measured} vehicle
($m=\SI{269.8}{\kilo\gram}$, $\mu_{\mathrm{peak}}=1.0$,
$v_{\max}\approx\SIrange{13}{14}{\meter\per\second}$) is the prerequisite for
the closed-loop experiment and is the immediate next step
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
