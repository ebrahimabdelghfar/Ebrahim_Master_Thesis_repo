% === VI. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS =============================================
\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

All experiments run on the architecture of Section~\ref{sec:platform}: the
Formula AI vehicle in CARLA~\cite{carla2017} driven through ROS~2 Humble, with
the server in synchronous mode at a \SI{0.033}{\second} fixed physics step. The
identifier collects \SI{30}{\second} of data at \SI{50}{\hertz} and
re-identifies from the newest buffer, matching the baseline's data
budget~\cite{ontrack2025}. Ground truth for forces, slip angles and normal
loads is the simulator's own per-wheel telemetry, which the identifier never
reads.

\subsubsection{Surface}
Every run reported here uses a \emph{static} friction surface: the per-wheel
coefficient is held at its configured value of \num{1.5} for the whole run, and
the road-surface factor of \num{0.70} that PhysX applies on top of it makes the
effective coefficient the identifier is scored against \num{1.05}, constant to
the last digit the simulator reports over all \num{8186} and \num{9355}
force-validation samples of the two runs. Time-varying friction is out of scope
here (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsubsection{Reference trajectory}
\label{subsubsec:raceline}
Both runs follow the same optimised raceline (Fig.~\ref{fig:raceline}),
produced with the TUM global race trajectory optimisation
package~\cite{tumracetraj} under its minimum-curvature
formulation~\cite{heilmeier2020} on a recorded centreline of the CARLA
silverstone circuit: a closed \SI{5828.2}{\meter} lap of \num{2916} waypoints
at \SI{2.0}{\meter} spacing, reference speed
\SIrange{9.62}{25.78}{\meter\per\second}, minimum corner radius
\SI{18.7}{\meter} ($|\kappa|_{\max}=\SI{0.0536}{\per\meter}$). Its peak lateral
demand is $\max(v_{x}^{2}|\kappa|)=\SI{4.98}{\meter\per\second\squared}$
(\SI{0.51}{\g}), which saturates the flat
\SI{5.0}{\meter\per\second\squared} \texttt{ggv} envelope the optimiser was
given and stays at roughly half the \SI{1.0}{\g} the plant sustains. This
trajectory replaces the earlier one, whose \SI{1.22}{\g} demand the
admissibility gate of Section~\ref{sec:integration} rejected against the same
plant; the regeneration, not a relaxed gate, is what unblocked the closed-loop
runs below.

\subsubsection{The two configurations}
\label{subsubsec:scenarios}
Two configurations of the same pipeline are run back to back on that
trajectory, three timed laps each, with an untimed out-lap from the spawn point
to the raceline's $s=0$. They differ in the four settings of
Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} and in nothing else: the vehicle, the map, the
trajectory, the controller, the data budget, the co-identification loop count
and the Pacejka box constraints are shared. \texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE} is the
inherited configuration---MLP residual, one-step MSE objective, local
trust-region fit, no friction warm start---evaluated at full scale on the
corrected rollout, so the comparison isolates the four settings rather than
reproducing the published pipeline in full (that comparison is
Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). \texttt{Ours} is the shipped configuration. Because
the four settings move together, the comparison below is a configuration
comparison and not a per-factor ablation. It is also not a test of this
paper's central contribution: the excitation-aware rollout of
Section~\ref{subsec:excitation} is active in \emph{both} arms, so what
Section~\ref{subsec:resultsclosedloop} measures is the four secondary settings
on top of that correction. The correction itself is evidenced in
Section~\ref{subsec:sweep}.

\subsubsection{What is measured}
Three families of quantity are recorded, each against the plant's own
telemetry: the identified tire model (coefficients, axle force, peak friction),
the one-step state prediction, and the closed-loop tracking of the MPC that
consumes the model. Every figure in this section is exported together with the
CSV holding the samples behind it, and the cross-run comparison reads those
CSVs rather than re-deriving any metric, so a figure and the table cannot
disagree.

\subsection{Identifiability of the peak factor against rollout excitation}
\label{subsec:sweep}

The identifiability claim of Section~\ref{subsec:excitation} is a claim about
the rollout configuration alone, so it is tested where the answer is known
exactly rather than in CARLA. A tire with a known peak factor $D$ is rolled out
through the synthetic steering sweep of stage~2---the shipped rollout, with the
residual network set to zero---and fitted back with the shipped solver. The fit
is run \emph{unregularised} and started from the true coefficients, so nothing
the fit loses is lost to the prior, to the start point or to a local minimum;
everything it loses is lost to excitation. Table~\ref{tab:sweep} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} report the grid.

Two axes are swept, rollout speed $v$ and true $D$, and each cell is repeated
over five noise realisations. The repetition is over noise and not over the
solver because the noise-free fit is exactly seed-invariant here: five
multi-start seeds return the same coefficients to four decimal places, and so
does a jitter width seven times wider, which is the direct measurement behind
the statement that the degeneracy is not a solver problem. The injected noise
is Gaussian on the rolled-out states at the measured one-step persistence RMSE
of the two CARLA runs, $\sigma_{v_{y}}=\SI{0.009}{\meter\per\second}$ and
$\sigma_{\omega}=\SI{0.005}{\radian\per\second}$
(Table~\ref{tab:comparison}), and is what the intervals in
Table~\ref{tab:sweep} are computed over.

The grid says three things. First, the inherited
\SI{4}{\meter\per\second} rollout is uninformative about $D$ at \emph{every}
grip level tested: a $0.70$, a $1.05$, a $1.40$ and a $1.80$ tire all come back
as $\hat D$ between \num{0.50} and \num{0.59}, i.e.\ the fit reports
$\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}$ and not the tire, with two to seven of the eight
coefficients on a bound. Second, the boundary is
$\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}\gtrsim D$ and not a speed: at
\SI{6}{\meter\per\second}, where $\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}=0.96$, the $0.70$ and
$1.05$ tires are recovered to within \SI{3}{\percent} while the $1.40$ and
$1.80$ tires are \SI{13}{\percent} and \SI{30}{\percent} short. Above
$\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}=1.7$ every tire is recovered to within \SI{4}{\percent}
with no systematic railing. Third, the failure is not the solver's: the
noise-free fit is invariant to the multi-start seed and to the jitter width, so
multi-start, a global solver or a better start point cannot recover a
coefficient the data does not contain---which is why
Section~\ref{subsec:fit} reports the extra starts and the global solver as
secondary. The one caveat is at the top of the sweep, where a rollout far past
the peak begins to over-report a low-grip tire ($\hat D=\num{0.92}$ for a
$0.70$ tire at \SI{20}{\meter\per\second}), because the Magic Formula's falling
branch is being fitted to data the plant's own curve does not produce; the
extrapolation bound of Section~\ref{subsec:iterative} is what keeps the shipped
configuration away from that regime.

\subsection{Identified tire model}
\label{subsec:resultstire}

Figure~\ref{fig:pacejka} puts both identified curves against the plant's own
curve~\eqref{eq:physx} and lists the coefficients. Two things are visible.

First, no coefficient of \texttt{Ours} rests on a box constraint, in any of its
six accepted identifications: $B=\num{7.076}/\num{7.873}$ inside $[4,20]$,
$C=\num{1.346}/\num{1.383}$ inside $[1.2,2.2]$, $D=\num{1.009}/\num{1.002}$
inside $[0.4,2.0]$ and $E=\num{-2.000}/\num{-1.024}$ inside $[-3,1]$. The
front $E$ landing on a round $\num{-2.000}$ is the configured prior, not a
bound: $E$ has essentially no data support here (the lap reaches
\SI{2.2}{\degree} of slip, and $E$ shapes only the region around and past the
peak), which is precisely the coefficient the frozen Tikhonov target of
\eqref{eq:fitobj} exists to hold at its nominal value. Reading it as an
identified quantity would be a mistake in either configuration; what the
comparison shows is that the regularised fit parks it at the prior while the
unregularised one moves it to $\num{-2.264}$ on no evidence, having started
the run with $E_{f}$ on the $\num{-3.0}$ bound.
\texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE} puts $D$ on its upper bound of \num{2.000} in two of
its six accepted identifications---the first and the last, so the run both
starts and ends on the bound---and $E_{f}$ on its lower bound of \num{-3.0} in
the first. In between it wanders over $D_{f}\in[\num{1.241},\num{2.000}]$
without settling. A peak factor of \num{2.000} against a
plant that realises \num{1.05} is not a \SI{90}{\percent} error in a tire
parameter so much as the box-edge signature of
Section~\ref{subsec:identifiability}: the coefficient is not identified, and
nothing in a per-coefficient bounds check distinguishes that from a fit.

Second, the price \texttt{Ours} pays is in the linear range. Every slope here
is \eqref{eq:stiffness} for an identified set and the closed form of
Table~\ref{tab:ref} for the plant, both at $\bar F_{z}=1$ and at the same
static axle loads $F_{z,f}=\SI{1373}{\newton}$, $F_{z,r}=\SI{1274}{\newton}$,
so the three are comparable. \texttt{Ours} gives
\SI{13.2}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} front and
\SI{13.9}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} rear against the plant's \num{20.4} and
\num{19.0}, i.e.\ \SI{35}{\percent} and \SI{27}{\percent} low, while
\texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE} gives \num{19.1} and \num{18.1}, \SI{6.5}{\percent}
and \SI{4.6}{\percent} low. The
understeer sign is correct in both ($l_{r}C_{r}>l_{f}C_{f}$), so both sets pass
the stability gate of Section~\ref{subsec:gates}, and only
\texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE}'s grip ceiling is a claim the plant cannot honour:
\eqref{eq:gripceiling} gives \SI{2.00}{\g} for it against \SI{1.01}{\g} for
\texttt{Ours} and $\approx\SI{1.0}{\g}$ measured.

The reason both effects can coexist is the slip-angle coverage of the lap. Over
the identification samples of either run, $P_{99}(|\alpha|)=\SI{0.038}{\radian}$
(\SI{2.2}{\degree}) and $\max|\alpha|=\SI{0.045}{\radian}$, against the
\SI{12.0}{\degree} at which the plant's curve first reaches its peak
(Table~\ref{tab:ref}). The measured lap therefore constrains the slope and says
almost nothing about the peak; only the synthetic rollout of
Section~\ref{subsec:excitation} reaches up the curve, and only the warm start
of Section~\ref{subsec:friction} carries independent information about $\mu$.

\subsection{Peak friction}
\label{subsec:resultsmu}

Figure~\ref{fig:muerror} reports the error of the identified peak factor
against the effective friction the plant reports per wheel, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:mutimeseries} shows how it evolves over the run. With the surface
static at $\mu=1.05$, \texttt{Ours} returns $D_{f}=\num{1.009}$ and
$D_{r}=\num{1.002}$ in \emph{every} one of its six accepted identifications:
the published peak factor is constant for the whole run, so its error is a
constant offset and RMSE, MAE and maximum absolute error coincide at
\num{0.041} and \num{0.048}. The warm start's floor does not bind on any cycle
here. \texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE} spans $[1.241,2.000]$ front and
$[1.358,2.000]$ rear over its six, starting and ending on the bound, giving
RMSE \num{0.762} and \num{0.772}: \numrange{16}{19}$\times$ worse
($0.762/0.041=\num{18.6}$ front, $0.772/0.048=\num{16.1}$ rear).

One qualification belongs on the \texttt{Ours} column: the corrected pipeline
\emph{preserves} a physically valid peak factor rather than sharpening one. The
settled value is its configured prior, measured on this vehicle, and the fit
does not move it at all---six identifications, six identical coefficient sets to
four decimal places. What the
comparison shows is that the same data, the same rollout and the same box
constraints drive the inherited configuration onto the bound instead, and that
the difference is worth \SI{0.99}{\g} of claimed grip.

\subsection{Axle lateral force}
\label{subsec:resultsforce}

Figures~\ref{fig:forcebars}--\ref{fig:forcetime} score the identified curve at
the measured $(\alpha,F_{z})$ against the plant's per-wheel lateral force. The
ordering reverses across axles: \texttt{Ours} is better at the rear
(\SI{40.8}{\newton} against \SI{50.1}{\newton} RMSE, $R^{2}$ \num{0.979}
against \num{0.970}) and worse at the front (\SI{70.7}{\newton} against
\SI{45.9}{\newton}, $R^{2}$ \num{0.944} against \num{0.978}). The error
histograms (Fig.~\ref{fig:forcehist}) are centred in both cases and differ in
width and tails: \texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE}'s maximum absolute error is
\SI{215}{\newton} front and \SI{223}{\newton} rear against \SI{138}{\newton}
and \SI{85}{\newton} for \texttt{Ours}, so its lower front RMSE is bought
with a tail \num{1.6}$\times$ heavier at the front and \num{2.6}$\times$ at the
rear. Where the force is largest each model errs in the
direction its initial slope predicts---\texttt{Ours} short, the inherited
configuration long.

This is the linear-range deficit of Section~\ref{subsec:resultstire} showing up
as force error, and it is the one axis on which the inherited configuration is
better. At \SI{2.2}{\degree} of slip both curves are far below their peaks, so
a peak factor on its box constraint costs nothing here while a
\SI{35}{\percent} slope deficit costs \SI{71}{\newton} of front-axle RMSE. The metric that
separates the two models is therefore not the force at the slip angles the lap
visits but the friction ceiling those forces do not probe---which is precisely
what a controller planning a speed profile consumes.

\label{subsec:resultsstate}

Level~1 of the validation protocol (Section~\ref{subsec:validation}) is
reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:staterms} against a persistence baseline---next state
equals last measured state---written by the same benchmark node.
\texttt{Ours} predicts yaw rate at \SI{0.032}{\radian\per\second} RMSE against
\texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE}'s \num{0.068} ($R^{2}$ \num{0.950} against
\num{0.788}), and lateral velocity marginally worse
(\SI{0.027}{\meter\per\second} against \num{0.022}, $R^{2}$ \num{0.988} against
\num{0.992}). The error histograms (Figs.~\ref{fig:statehistvy}
and~\ref{fig:statehistomega}) show the yaw-rate difference as tail mass:
maximum absolute error \SI{0.452}{\radian\per\second} for \texttt{Ours} against
\SI{1.030}{\radian\per\second}.

Neither model beats persistence at the \SI{0.035}{\second} model step: the
skill scores $1-\mathrm{RMSE}/\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{pers}}$ are negative for
both configurations and both signals, worst at \num{-12.6}

\subsection{Closed-loop tracking}
\label{subsec:resultsclosedloop}

Level~3 of the validation protocol completes on the regenerated trajectory.
Both runs finish three timed laps with zero emergency halts and the same
finite-state sequence---bootstrap pure pursuit, running pure pursuit, handover,
running MPC---with one transition of each kind and no fallback to pure pursuit
after handover. The sequence is read off each run's exported state timeline
(\texttt{graphs/control/*/fsm\_\allowbreak state\_\allowbreak timeline.csv}),
which records the handover at $t=\SI{81.4}{\second}$ for \texttt{Ours} and
$t=\SI{66.0}{\second}$ for \texttt{Baseline-NN-MSE} and no state change
thereafter; the aggregated \texttt{transitions} column of the cross-run summary
is not populated and is omitted from Table~\ref{tab:comparison}. Under MPC, which is where the identified model enters the
loop, \texttt{Ours} tracks at \SI{0.092}{\meter} RMS lateral error against
\SI{0.177}{\meter}, a \SI{48}{\percent} reduction, with maximum
\SI{0.490}{\meter} against \SI{0.696}{\meter}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:trackingbox}). Overall ITAE is \num{10362} against \num{17651}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:itae}). ITAE is $\int t|e_{y}|\,\mathrm{d}t$ and is neither
scale-free nor comparable across runs of different length, and these two runs
are \SI{591.4}{\second} and \SI{564.1}{\second} long, so it is reported here
beside two normalised forms computed from the same samples: mean absolute
error $\mathrm{IAE}/T$, \SI{0.085}{\meter} against \SI{0.132}{\meter}, and
$\mathrm{ITAE}/(T^{2}/2)$, \SI{0.059}{\meter} against \SI{0.111}{\meter}. All
three order the runs the same way and at the same magnitude, so the
normalisation does not carry the result. The ITAE split confirms that the whole
difference is
in the MPC phase: the pure-pursuit phases, which do not use the identified
model, give \num{211} and \num{122} respectively, ordered the other way and two
orders of magnitude smaller. The signed error distribution
(Fig.~\ref{fig:eyhist}) shows the mechanism as a narrower distribution about a
comparable centre---sample standard deviation \SI{0.279}{\meter} against
\SI{0.301}{\meter}, mean \SI{0.014}{\meter} against \SI{0.039}{\meter}.

The QP cost is unchanged: RMS solve time \SI{0.413}{\milli\second} against
\SI{0.390}{\milli\second}, worst case \SI{3.81}{\milli\second} against
\SI{4.06}{\milli\second}, both two orders of magnitude below the
\SI{50}{\milli\second} control period, and the two orderings disagree, which
is what ``unchanged'' means here. The identified coefficients change the model the QP carries, not its
size.

Lap time is not read as a result. Timing runs on the ROS wall clock while the
simulator advances its fixed step faster than real time, and the ratio between
the two differed between the runs, so the recorded lap times in
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} are not simulated lap times and their
\SIrange{4}{6}{\percent} spread is not a vehicle-performance difference.

\subsection{Peak-friction warm start on a synthetic plant}
\label{subsec:mu}

The friction warm start of Section~\ref{subsec:friction} is evaluated on a
synthetic single-track plant with brush tires rather than in CARLA, because
only a synthetic plant supplies a $\mu$ that is known exactly and an excitation
level that can be swept independently of it. The plant is driven by a
swept-sine steering input for \SI{40}{\second} at \SI{50}{\hertz} with true
$\mu=1.05$ and $C_{\alpha}=12.1F_{z}$, and the steering amplitude sets how far
up the tire curve the manoeuvre reaches. Table~\ref{tab:mu} reports what each
estimator returns.

cycles (Fig.~\ref{fig:mutimeseries}); its contribution there is that it cannot
lower $D$, not that it raises it.

\subsection{Identification cost}
\label{subsec:runtime}

Section~\ref{subsec:s4cost}'s claim is that the temporal residual is affordable
on the hardware a vehicle carries; Table~\ref{tab:runtime} is the measurement,
on a synthetic \SI{30}{\second} lap and the same six co-identification
iterations throughout. The per-stage rows and the end-to-end totals were
measured separately and are not a decomposition of each other: the stage rows
reconstruct \SI{156.2}{\second} of the \SI{181.4}{\second} before and
\SI{21.1}{\second} of the \SI{20.9}{\second} after, so the residual
\SI{25.2}{\second} of fixed cost visible before is not visible after. We
report both as measured rather than reconciling them by attribution. Every
step was verified against the implementation it replaced before being kept: the convolutional and recurrent paths of
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipelines4}(c) agree to $<10^{-9}$ in double precision (pinned by
a regression test), the rewritten physics-informed loss is bit-identical, and
the full six-iteration loop lands on the same Pacejka coefficients to four
decimal places---the remaining difference is float32 reordering over
\num{1200} optimiser steps.

Two consequences follow. At \SI{20.9}{\second} the identification fits inside
the \SI{30}{\second} re-identification period of
Section~\ref{subsec:deployment}, so the temporal residual is a deployable
option rather than an offline one; at \SI{181.4}{\second} it was not. And the
device split is not incidental: the stage-2 rollout is strictly sequential and
single-sample, so it is \num{4.4}$\times$ \emph{faster} on a CPU, while
full-batch training over $\approx\num{3000}$ windows remains
\num{2.4}$\times$ faster on the GPU. A pipeline pinned to one device pays one
of those two penalties.
