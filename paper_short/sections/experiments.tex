% === VI. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS =============================================
\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

All experiments run on the architecture of Section~\ref{sec:platform}: the
Formula AI vehicle in CARLA driven through ROS~2 Humble, the server in
synchronous mode at a \SI{0.033}{\second} fixed physics step. The identifier
collects \SI{30}{\second} of data at \SI{50}{\hertz} and re-identifies from the
newest buffer, matching the baseline's data budget~\cite{ontrack2025}. Ground
truth is the simulator's own per-wheel telemetry, which the identifier never
reads; every figure is exported with the CSV holding its samples, and the
cross-run comparison reads those CSVs rather than re-deriving any metric.

\subsubsection{Surface}
Every run uses a \emph{static} surface: the per-wheel coefficient is held at its
configured \num{1.0}, and the road-surface factor of \num{0.70} PhysX applies on
top of it makes the effective coefficient \num{0.70}, constant to the last digit
the simulator reports over all \num{10744} and \num{12025} force-validation
samples of the two runs. It is deliberately \emph{not} the $\mu=\num{1.05}$
surface the tire prior was measured on
(Section~\ref{subsubsec:priorprovenance}), so a pipeline that cannot move off
its prior has nowhere to hide. It remains a \emph{single} surface: the
adaptivity claim a friction sweep would support is not made
(Section~\ref{subsec:limits}).

\subsubsection{Reference trajectory}
\label{subsubsec:raceline}
Both runs follow the same optimised raceline (Fig.~\ref{fig:raceline}),
generated with the TUM global race trajectory optimisation
package~\cite{tumracetraj} under its minimum-curvature
formulation~\cite{heilmeier2020}: a closed \SI{5828.2}{\meter} lap of
\num{2916} waypoints, reference speed
\SIrange{9.62}{25.78}{\meter\per\second}, minimum corner radius
\SI{18.7}{\meter} ($|\kappa|_{\max}=\SI{0.0536}{\per\meter}$). Its peak lateral
demand is $\max(v_{x}^{2}|\kappa|)=\SI{4.98}{\meter\per\second\squared}$
(\SI{0.51}{\g}), roughly \SI{73}{\percent} of the \SI{0.70}{\g} this surface
sustains. It replaces an earlier trajectory whose \SI{1.22}{\g} demand the
admissibility gate of Section~\ref{sec:integration} rejected against the same
plant; that regeneration, not a relaxed gate, unblocked the closed-loop runs.

\subsubsection{The two configurations}
\label{subsubsec:scenarios}
Two configurations of the same pipeline are run back to back on that
trajectory, three timed laps each, with an untimed out-lap. They differ in the
five settings of Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} and in nothing else; vehicle, map,
trajectory, controller, data budget, loop count and box constraints are shared.
\basearm{} is the inherited configuration evaluated at full scale
on the corrected rollout, so the comparison isolates those five settings rather
than reproducing the published pipeline in full; \oursarm{} is the shipped
configuration. Since the five settings move together, what follows is a
configuration comparison, not a per-factor ablation, which
Section~\ref{subsec:ablations} supplies offline. Nor is it a test of this
paper's central contribution: the excitation-aware rollout is active in
\emph{both} arms, and the correction itself is evidenced in
Section~\ref{subsec:sweep}.

\subsubsection{Provenance of the tire prior}
\label{subsubsec:priorprovenance}
The prior $\phip^{0}$ both arms start from, and that the Tikhonov term of
\eqref{eq:fitobj} pulls toward, is the measured tire of Table~\ref{tab:ref},
obtained by fitting \eqref{eq:mf} to CARLA's own per-wheel telemetry over a
dedicated limit-excitation run that reached $\alpha=\SI{0.37}{\radian}$, axle
$\mu=\num{1.05}$ and \SI{1.50}{\g}. It is not independent of the validation
data, since that is the same channel the force and friction results are scored
against; the manoeuvre that produced it is one the pipeline is not allowed to
run, the raceline peaking at \SI{0.51}{\g}; and, decisively, it was measured on
a surface these runs do not use, carrying $D=\num{1.009}/\num{1.002}$ and axle
slopes of \SI{13.2}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} front and
\SI{13.9}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} rear against the \num{0.70} and the
\num{17.4} and \SI{16.6}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} this surface realises.
Returning it unchanged is a \SI{44}{\percent} grip overstatement and a
\SIrange{16}{24}{\percent} stiffness deficit; to score well an arm has to
\emph{move}, in the right direction, from a prior that is wrong.

\subsection{Identifiability of the peak factor against rollout excitation}
\label{subsec:sweep}

The claim of Section~\ref{subsec:excitation} concerns the rollout configuration
alone, so it is tested where the answer is known exactly rather than in CARLA. A
tire with a known peak factor $D$ is rolled out through the synthetic sweep of
stage~2 (the shipped rollout, residual network set to zero) and fitted back with
the shipped solver, \emph{unregularised} and started from the true coefficients,
so everything it loses is lost to excitation. Rollout speed $v$, sweep amplitude
$\delta_{\mathrm{sweep}}$ and true $D$ are swept, each cell repeated over five
noise realisations at the one-step persistence RMSE measured on CARLA runs of
this vehicle. The configuration axes are swept one at a time, because
$\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}$ is a function of both; the repetition is over noise rather
than the solver because the noise-free fit is exactly seed-invariant here.

The grid says three things. First, the inherited \SI{4}{\meter\per\second}
rollout is uninformative about $D$ at \emph{every} grip level tested: the
$0.70$, $1.05$, $1.40$ and $1.80$ tires all come back as $\hat D$ between
\num{0.50} and \num{0.59}, with two to seven of the eight coefficients on a
bound, so the fit reports $\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}$ and not the tire. Second, the
boundary is $\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}\gtrsim D$ and not a speed: at
\SI{6}{\meter\per\second}, where $\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}=0.96$, the $0.70$ and
$1.05$ tires are recovered to within \SI{3}{\percent} while the $1.40$ and
$1.80$ tires are \SI{13}{\percent} and \SI{30}{\percent} short; above
$\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}=1.7$ every tire is recovered to within \SI{4}{\percent}
with no systematic railing. The bound predicts where the information runs out,
not a step. Third, the failure is not the solver's: multi-start, a global solver
or a better start point cannot recover a coefficient the data does not contain.

Panel (b) sweeps the other argument of \eqref{eq:mureach} at fixed
$v=\SI{15}{\meter\per\second}$. Starving the amplitude destroys $D$ just as
starving the speed does, so speed is not the governing variable, and the
direction of the error is set by the rollout rather than the tire: an
under-speeded rollout returns \emph{too little} grip, an under-swept one too
much (the \num{1.05} tire at \num{1.30}, the \num{1.80} tire railed on the
\num{2.0} bound at $\delta_{\mathrm{sweep}}=\SI{0.06}{\radian}$). That is the
signature of an unidentified parameter, not a biased one. The axes do \emph{not}
collapse onto a common $\mu_{\mathrm{reach}}$, for the upper-bound reason of
Section~\ref{subsec:excitation}: at $v=\SI{20}{\meter\per\second}$
\eqref{eq:mureach} reads \num{10.64} against a realised \numrange{1.40}{2.18}.
Read against the realised demand both axes behave alike, so \eqref{eq:mureach}
should be used as the pipeline uses it --- a cheap screening bound, conclusive
when it falls below $D$, not a measure of excitation achieved. One property
bounds how all of this should be read: with the residual set to zero, a Magic
Formula is fitted to Magic-Formula data under exactly matched structure, so the
recovery is a best case.

\subsection{Identified tire model}
\label{subsec:resultstire}

Figure~\ref{fig:pacejka} puts both identified curves against the plant's own
curve~\eqref{eq:physx} and lists the coefficients. Three things are visible.

First, no coefficient of \oursarm{} rests on a box constraint in any of its
six accepted identifications; its last accepted set is
$B=\num{12.890}/\num{13.746}$ inside $[4,20]$, $C=\num{1.240}/\num{1.248}$
inside $[1.2,2.2]$, $D=\num{0.768}$ on both axles inside $[0.4,2.0]$ and
$E=\num{-2.517}/\num{-1.836}$ inside $[-3,1]$. \basearm{} puts $D$
on its upper bound of \num{2.000} on both axles in \emph{every} accepted
identification and $E_{f}$ on its lower bound of \num{-3.0} likewise. A peak
factor of \num{2.000} against a plant realising \num{0.70} is less a
\SI{186}{\percent} parameter error than the box-edge signature of
Section~\ref{subsec:identifiability}: the coefficient is not identified, and no
per-coefficient bounds check distinguishes that from a fit.

Second, the linear range is identified rather than inherited. Every slope is
\eqref{eq:stiffness} for an identified set; for the plant it is the slope fitted
to the simulator's own per-wheel forces at the static axle loads
$F_{z,f}=\SI{1373}{\newton}$, $F_{z,r}=\SI{1274}{\newton}$, which realises
\SI{17.4}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} front and \SI{16.6}{\kilo\newton\per\radian}
rear. The prior carries \num{13.2} and \num{13.9}, \SI{24}{\percent} and
\SI{16}{\percent} low; \oursarm{} returns \num{16.9} and \num{16.8},
\SI{3.2}{\percent} low front and \SI{1.4}{\percent} high rear, removing
\SI{87}{\percent} and \SI{91}{\percent} of the prior's error. The product
$BCDF_{z}$, the only quantity the rollout fit determines
(Section~\ref{subsec:bcd}), is what moves and then holds: the five cycles after
the friction gate releases sit within
\SIrange{16.0}{16.9}{\kilo\newton\per\radian}, a \SI{5}{\percent} spread about a
plant at \num{17.4}. \basearm{} returns \num{37.9} and \num{38.4},
\SI{118}{\percent} and \SI{132}{\percent} high, which is $D=\num{2.0}$
multiplying a $B$ the fit chose against it.

Third, both sets keep the understeer sign and pass the stability gate of
Section~\ref{subsec:gates}; what separates them is the grip ceiling, which
\eqref{eq:gripceiling} puts at \SI{2.00}{\g} for \basearm{}
against \SI{0.77}{\g} for \oursarm{}, a plant sustaining \SI{0.71}{\g} and a
trajectory demanding \SI{0.51}{\g}. The baseline's ceiling is a claim the plant
cannot honour by a factor of \num{2.8}; only the identified set is admissible on
the quantity the controller consumes. All three readings are possible because
the lap now traverses part of the curvature:
$P_{99}(|\alpha|)=\SI{2.7}{\degree}$ and $\max|\alpha|=\SI{5.8}{\degree}$
against the \SI{8.0}{\degree} at which the plant's curve peaks, at an axle
utilisation of \num{0.71} front and \num{0.72} rear. This is still not a limit
manoeuvre, but it suffices for the friction gate to release, which on the
$\mu=\num{1.05}$ runs it did not.

\subsection{Peak friction}
\label{subsec:resultsmu}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} reports the error of the identified peak factor
against the effective friction the plant reports per wheel, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:mutimeseries} its evolution over the run. This is where the
pinned-$D$ design of Section~\ref{subsec:friction} is directly observable,
because the arm starts \SI{44}{\percent} away from the answer.
\oursarm{} publishes six accepted identifications. The first carries
$D_{f}=\num{1.009}$, $D_{r}=\num{1.002}$: the friction gate rejected that
buffer, so the axles held the prior they came in with, the hold-rather-than-fit
rule of Section~\ref{subsec:frictionfloor} and not a fitted value. From
$t=\SI{140.6}{\second}$ the gate releases on every subsequent cycle and the
published peak factor drops to \num{0.779}, \num{0.821}, \num{0.749},
\num{0.756} and \num{0.768}, front and rear alike, against a plant at
\num{0.700}: \SI{10.7}{\percent} high on average, from a prior that was
\SI{44}{\percent} high. The whole-run $\mu$ RMSE is dominated by the first
\SI{140}{\second} at the prior; over the \SI{440}{\second} after the first
release it is \num{0.078} on both axles. \basearm{} returns
$D=\num{2.000}$ on both axles in every accepted identification and never leaves
the bound, a constant $\mu$ error of \num{1.300}, so \oursarm{} is
\num{9.0}$\times$ better on the whole-run figure and \num{16.7}$\times$ better
once its warm start has released --- a difference worth \SI{1.23}{\g} of claimed
grip against a plant that sustains \num{0.71}. Two qualifications belong on that
column: the peak factor is \emph{measured} by the brush estimator on the
recorded trace and then held, not recovered by the Pacejka fit, which
Section~\ref{subsec:bcd} shows cannot recover it; and the residual
\SI{10.7}{\percent} bias is one-signed, consistent with the brush model's
tendency to over-read $\mu$ where the curve is not fully traversed.

\subsection{Axle lateral force and one-step state prediction}
\label{subsec:resultsforce}

The identified curve is scored at the measured $(\alpha,F_{z})$ against the
plant's own lateral force, summed per axle (Table~\ref{tab:comparison};
Fig.~\ref{fig:errhist}(a)). \oursarm{} leads on both axles by an order of
magnitude in RMSE and in the error tails; the baseline's negative $R^{2}$ means
its curve predicts the plant's axle force worse than the mean of that force
does. This is the linear-range result seen as force: at
$P_{99}(|\alpha|)=\SI{2.7}{\degree}$ the metric is dominated by cornering
stiffness, so the models separate almost entirely on slope --- but because $D$
multiplies that slope in \eqref{eq:stiffness}, \basearm{}'s railed
$D$ is the direct cause of its force error. The comparison is also why the
relaxed update of \eqref{eq:relax} belongs in the method rather than a
configuration file: pinning $D$ alone does not produce these numbers, since with
$\beta=1$ the same arm degrades monotonically with iteration count
(Section~\ref{subsec:ablations}).

Level~1 of the validation protocol is reported against a persistence baseline
(next state equals last measured state) written by the same benchmark node.
\oursarm{} leads on both signals, by an order of magnitude on lateral
velocity ($R^{2}$ \num{0.998} against \num{0.709}) and by a factor of two on yaw
rate ($R^{2}$ \num{0.894} against \num{0.514}); in
Fig.~\ref{fig:errhist}(b)--(c) the separation on $v_{y}$ is in the centre of the
distribution, while on $\omega$ the two share a centre and differ in tail mass.
Neither model beats persistence at the \SI{0.035}{\second} model step, the skill
scores being negative for both configurations and both signals, because a
one-step metric on a smooth trajectory rewards not moving. One-step accuracy is
therefore weak evidence for either tire model, and the gap is read only as
confirming the direction the axle-force and closed-loop comparisons establish.

\subsection{Closed-loop tracking}
\label{subsec:resultsclosedloop}

Both runs finish three timed laps with zero emergency halts and the same
finite-state sequence, with the handover at $t=\SI{79.1}{\second}$ for
\oursarm{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:speedcost}) and $t=\SI{64.0}{\second}$ for
\basearm{}. Under MPC, where the identified model enters the loop,
\oursarm{} tracks at \SI{0.151}{\meter} RMS lateral error against
\SI{0.224}{\meter}, a \SI{32}{\percent} reduction, with maximum
\SI{0.683}{\meter} against \SI{0.776}{\meter} (Fig.~\ref{fig:trackingbox}). ITAE
is $\int t|e_{y}|\,\mathrm{d}t$, neither scale-free nor comparable across runs
of different length, so the duration-normalised forms
$\mathrm{IAE}/T=\num{0.104}$ against \num{0.157} and
$\mathrm{ITAE}/(T^{2}/2)=\num{0.095}$ against \num{0.166} are reported beside
it; all three order the runs alike. The ITAE split confirms that the whole
difference sits in the MPC phase: the pure-pursuit phases, which do not use the
identified model, give \num{110} and \num{107}, within \SI{3}{\percent} of each
other and two orders of magnitude smaller. The QP cost is unchanged: RMS and
worst-case solve times are two orders of magnitude below the
\SI{50}{\milli\second} control period (Fig.~\ref{fig:speedcost}) and both
slightly in the baseline's favour, by \SI{5}{\percent} and \SI{1}{\percent}. The
identified coefficients change the model the QP carries, not its size. Lap time
is not read as a result: timing runs on the ROS wall clock while the simulator
advances its fixed step faster than real time, at a ratio that differed between
the runs, so the \SIrange{5}{6}{\percent} lap-time spread is not a
vehicle-performance difference.

\subsection{What carries the result}
\label{subsec:ablations}

The two arms differ in five settings at once, so that comparison establishes an
ordering and attributes nothing. This subsection attributes it, at the cost of
leaving the loop: every number below is an \emph{offline replay} of the
identification stage on the five \SI{30}{\second} buffers each arm collected
during its own run, scored with the quantity the closed-loop tire-force
benchmark itself reports, so these numbers and Table~\ref{tab:comparison}'s are
on the same scale.

\subsubsection{Damping the co-identification loop}
Table~\ref{tab:beta} sweeps $\beta$ of \eqref{eq:relax} at six iterations
throughout; two columns are the argument. At $\beta=\num{1.00}$, the inherited
undamped update, axle force RMSE is \num{70.4} and \SI{62.3}{\newton}, reached
by degrading monotonically from \num{38.9} and \SI{26.3}{\newton} at a single
iteration: the loop is not refining the estimate, it is amplifying its own
extrapolation. At $\beta=\num{0.00}$ the coefficients never move from the pinned
prior --- no identification at all --- and that is the \emph{worst} column,
\num{92.5} and \SI{71.8}{\newton}. The minimum sits between them at
$\beta=\num{0.20}$, \num{28.0} and \SI{19.4}{\newton}, a mean of
\SI{23.7}{\newton}, and the identified $BCD$ rises monotonically with $\beta$,
so the damping trades how much of each fit is accepted against how much
extrapolation error comes with it. Six iterations are therefore defensible on
evidence, but only damped.

\subsubsection{The other three settings}
Pinning the peak factor accounts for most of what remains. Replayed on five
buffers of a $\mu=\num{1.05}$ run, letting the fit move $D$ gives a mean
$|D-\mu|$ of \num{0.585} and a front-stiffness spread of
\SI{32.9}{\kilo\newton\per\radian}; pinning only the axles whose brush fit was
released reduces those to \num{0.191} and \num{32.3}, and additionally holding
the axles with no released measurement, the shipped rule, to \num{0.084} and
\SI{18.5}{\kilo\newton\per\radian} --- so what removes most of the error is
refusing to let an unmeasured $D$ be fitted at all. Normalising the
physics-informed objective is necessary but not sufficient alone: it moves the
loss shares from \SI{99.9996}{\percent} on the dynamics residual to
\SI{1.47}{\percent}, yet applied without the damping it makes the force metric
slightly \emph{worse} (\num{77.1} and \SI{76.2}{\newton} against \num{70.4} and
\SI{62.3}{\newton}), because an objective that now fits the data supplies a
sharper trajectory for the undamped loop to amplify. Two settings deserve a
weaker statement than the closed-loop comparison alone would support: the S4D
residual is worth \SI{21}{\percent} on the force metric at matched settings and
a single iteration (\SI{35.3}{\newton} against the baseline MLP's
\SI{44.7}{\newton}), an offline replay and not a closed-loop result; and the
residual \emph{objective} is a wash, \texttt{physics\_informed} and plain
\texttt{mse} being indistinguishable at \SI{23.7}{\newton} each once the
normalisation and the damping are both in place. We keep it because it costs
nothing and is the paper's stated formulation, not because it buys accuracy.

\subsection{Identification cost}
\label{subsec:runtime}

One S4D identification, six co-identification iterations on a \SI{30}{\second}
lap, takes \SI{20.9}{\second} end to end, inside the \SI{30}{\second}
re-identification period. The reduction from the \SI{181.4}{\second} it
originally took is exact: no epoch count, iteration count, early-stopping rule
or loss weight changed, and the identified coefficients agree to four decimal
places. The per-phase breakdown is in the supplementary material.
